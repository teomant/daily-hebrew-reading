# Short everyday-item instructions

Plan compact, fully AI-generated everyday situations for a single daily SHORTS page. Each returned record is one independent mini-situation that will be adapted separately and later collected with the other items on one page.

Use tiny situations with reusable Hebrew across the three supplied pools.
Explore different things people do, not just different things they ask a service worker.
An item may be neutral or end happily, but it needs a concrete action, question, or result.
Skip context-free pleasant moments.

Before writing, compare the supplied recent scenarios and already selected stories across EVERYDAY, DIALOG, and SHORTS.
Notice overused situations and plot patterns, then choose less-used activities within the allowed domains and pool targets.
Different domain labels do not guarantee different reading experiences.
Changing the object, workplace, or question in “customer asks, worker explains, customer confirms” does not provide enough variety for a collection.
Do not shorten a recent full story into a short item or expand a recent short item into another version of the same situation.

Build the collection from different actions, relationships, and outcomes.
People can offer help, decline an invitation, lend something, divide a task, compare preferences, admit a mistake, return a favor, disagree politely, demonstrate a skill, improvise with available materials, or react to a small surprise.
For example, someone catches a falling bag while its owner opens a door; two colleagues exchange tasks because one needs a quiet space; a cook rescues an over-salted dish; friends disagree about a film scene; a child corrects an adult's mistaken assumption.
These are starting points for breadth, not a checklist or recurring assignments.
Create other situations rather than cycling through these examples.
Use friends, relatives, colleagues, neighbors, strangers, and people acting on their own as well as customers and staff.
Useful Hebrew also includes offers, refusals, explanations, reactions, opinions, apologies, and humor that fits an ordinary situation.
Do not make most items service questions, requests for directions, checks of forms or screens, or requests for permission.
Let some people take initiative, cooperate, negotiate, notice something, or make a choice without asking an authority.

Before returning the collection, mentally remove names, locations, and object names from each brief.
If two items still have essentially the same goal, action, and result, replace one with a different situation.
Apply this comparison to recent records too, even when the scenario identifiers differ.
Keep repeated useful vocabulary, but vary what actually happens.
Do not output this comparison process.

Each brief must describe one tiny concrete situation, one useful interaction or action, and its immediate answer or result. It must support one compact Hebrew paragraph of exactly two or three complete sentences. Do not add background, a second event, or a full EVERYDAY story arc. Do not write Hebrew in this planning phase.

These are no-web fictional situations. Do not assert current fees, deadlines, eligibility, coverage, official procedures, or diagnoses. Use ordinary fictional details and show the actual immediate answer or action. A short item can ask how to verify a detail when that fits, but do not make referrals to instructions the repeated answer across the page. Vary observations, requests, choices, small misunderstandings, and shared actions. An unresolved official question can coexist with a concrete ordinary action; do not invent a rule to provide closure.

Use only the canonical domains supplied in the request. Scenarios in the same collection may share a broad domain when their actual interaction and outcome differ. Do not repeat or cosmetically rename a recent scenario. Provide the same metadata as EVERYDAY, with an empty `dialogSpeakers` list, no sources, and no image.
