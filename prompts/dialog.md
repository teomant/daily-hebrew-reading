# Dialogue instructions

Plan fully AI-generated DIALOG scenarios for learners who want to use natural Hebrew in essential situations, routine life, and worthwhile experiences in Israel. This phase creates only English briefs and scenario metadata; do not research factual stories, use web sources, write Hebrew adaptations, segment text, or translate anything. A speaker should need to explain something, ask a useful question, clarify an answer, correct a misunderstanding, make a decision, or agree on a next step. Use varied settings from the supplied domain pools, including services and institutions, homes and kitchens, transport, trips, and events. These examples are not a fixed list.

Plan each dialogue as a normal conversation between Israelis, not a lesson, dramatic scene, or narrated story.
Usually use two speakers.
Plan enough interaction for 8–12 short alternating turns at every reading level, with each speaker turn later rendered on its own line.
Keep the situation simple and useful, with questions, answers, clarifications, reactions, and a clear practical outcome.
Avoid dramatic plots, artificial repetition, exaggerated slang, and vocabulary included only to teach a rule.

Use the canonical domain values supplied in the request and a different domain for each generated story in the current issue.
Vary practical goals: clarify a bill, return an item, explain a work task, arrange childcare, or find the correct bus stop.
Include situations beyond these examples and follow the supplied pool priorities.
A positive exchange can carry a dialogue when it leads to a concrete action or outcome.
Do not fill eight turns with abstract plans to start a hobby or make a shared album.
Avoid repeating the same delay, missing-item, or phone-call plot under new names.

These no-web dialogues teach reusable conversation, not current official guidance. Do not invent exact fees, rates, deadlines, eligibility, coverage, medical diagnoses, or entitlements. Use ordinary fictional observations, options, explanations, and actions to make the conversation specific. Refer to official instructions only when the question actually requires them, not as the automatic answer to every problem. Show the useful answer itself: “we read it and now it is clear” is not an explanation. If an official decision remains open, speakers can still resolve an ordinary practical detail without inventing a rule.

Keep speaker roles stable, including who pays whom, who provides the service, and who owns or needs something. Vary the exchanges across the batch: clarification, preference, disagreement, discovery, and cooperation should not all become the same request for instructions.
Plan the actual conversational need and answer, not just a sequence of requests to check instructions.
Each reply should follow from the preceding turn, using the kind of direct, ordinary wording people use in shops, at work, and at home.
An unfamiliar term may need a simple explanation, but the conversation must not sound like a language lesson or a formal interview unless the situation is an actual interview.

The English scenario brief must identify the speakers, their relationship when relevant, the practical situation, what they need to decide or solve, the important clarification or reaction, and the outcome. Put exactly two short Hebrew first names in `dialogSpeakers`; these are the binding labels for every adapted level. Plan enough distinct questions, answers, follow-ups, reactions, and decision points to support 8–12 useful turns without repetition or artificially long speeches. It must support direct speech rather than prose describing that people talked. If the idea is mainly narration, classify it as EVERYDAY rather than DIALOG.

DIALOG stories use the same scenario metadata as EVERYDAY stories and have no sources or image. Treat every supplied recent scenario record as forbidden comparison data. Never return an identical `scenario` value, and do not rename a recent scenario while keeping substantially the same practical problem or goal, interaction, and resolution. Published dialogues must be visibly marked as fully AI-generated.

Before freezing the brief, map each of the two Hebrew names to an English role and responsibilities.
State who needs help, who can answer, who owns each object, and who performs the final action.
Use only those two active participants.
If a server must explain the bill, make the server one of the two speakers rather than making two diners report the server's words.
Plan the order of questions and answers so neither person changes roles halfway through.
Include `everydayMeta.storyPattern` with compact English goal, relationship, interaction, and outcome phrases.
Compare patterns after removing names and replaceable objects.
