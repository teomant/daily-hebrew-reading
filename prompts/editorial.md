# Sourced discovery instructions

Find factual CURRENT and HISTORY material for a practical spoken-Hebrew magazine. This phase only discovers and freezes supported English briefs and source metadata. Do not plan EVERYDAY or DIALOG stories, write Hebrew, choose reading-level wording, segment text, or translate anything.

Language value is the primary selection criterion: useful frequent vocabulary, relevance to conversations learners may actually have, a clear central idea, then freshness. On the first discovery attempt, search across the whole of Israel and varied cities, regions, and communities. Do not default to Jerusalem, and do not let one city dominate merely because its English-language sources are easy to find. When the user prompt marks the request as a retry, treat it as a worldwide replacement search rather than another Israel-first pass. Use new queries, search varied countries and regions, and make most candidates non-Israeli. Israeli stories remain allowed only when genuinely new.

CURRENT should focus on practical developments from the target date or previous several days that people might naturally discuss: food and prices, shops, services, public transport, workplaces, housing, appointments, schools, consumer rules, local culture, and understandable human stories. Prefer concrete effects on residents over national headline summaries. Technology is low priority and is suitable only when it directly changes how ordinary people use a common phone, app, payment, workplace, or service.

Reject stories whose main value is spectacle rather than everyday language: distant natural disasters, rescue missions, space hardware, specialist science, product-industry announcements, and technical business deals. A story being surprising or widely reported is not enough. Select it only if learners could realistically reuse much of its vocabulary in normal conversation.

Exclude party politics, elections, politicians as protagonists, parliamentary or coalition disputes, ideological conflict, war, military operations, terrorism, geopolitics, diplomatic conflict, serious crime, murder, mass tragedy, outrage bait, and stories that require extensive political context.

For HISTORY on the first attempt, look first for worthwhile Israeli facts connected to the target date or recent days, but never force a date connection. Build a broad pool led by three families: real past events with a clear human sequence and consequence; notable people such as artists, writers, scientists, educators, engineers, athletes, founders, guides, and community figures; and the histories of Israeli nature sites, national parks, gardens, trails, viewpoints, museums, landmarks, unusual local attractions, and tourist destinations. Include at least four candidates from each family and use the remaining HISTORY candidates for the strongest varied subjects. Streets, neighborhoods, buildings, stations, markets, institutions, food, customs, everyday objects, transport routes, local businesses, and ordinary practices remain useful when they have a distinctive story. Broader Israeli history is acceptable when it is easy to tell and supplies reusable vocabulary. On a retry, equally consider short, concrete, relatable history from anywhere in the world. Do not relabel current reporting as HISTORY.

Archaeology is a fallback, not the default meaning of HISTORY. Do not let excavation reports, ancient streets, building layers, pottery finds, or construction-site discoveries dominate the candidate pool. Return no more than two candidates whose central hook is an archaeological dig, and rank diverse events, people, nature sites, parks, interesting places, and destinations ahead of repetitive excavation stories. Different place names do not make the repeated “archaeologists uncovered another street or building” pattern editorially diverse.

Apply a strict recent-subject exclusion before returning anything. For HISTORY, the same named street, building, archaeological site, institution, person, event, object, custom, or other primary subject used in a forbidden or already selected story is a duplicate. A different source, excavation report, historical period, archaeological layer, newly emphasized fact, or angle about that same primary subject does not make it new. For CURRENT, a later status report, continuing consequence, “still” update, new article, or changed statistic about the same underlying event remains a duplicate. Discard such candidates and continue searching for a genuinely different primary subject or event.

Every candidate needs a compact but adaptation-ready factual brief supported by at least one consulted source page. Include the central event or historical subject plus enough supported specifics to sustain a developed 4–5-paragraph learner article: who or what is involved, where and when it happened when relevant, the useful sequence, cause or background, practical effect, reaction, and outcome. Include only dimensions the consulted source actually supports. Never invent names, quotations, reactions, numbers, causes, dates, or outcomes. When evidence is thin, include less and accept that the later adaptation may be shorter. Use real publisher names, article titles, and canonical HTTPS URLs. Every source URL must be unique and point to the specific content page used. Never use a publisher homepage, section/category/tag page, search page, generic “latest” page, or rolling liveblog.

Return exactly 28 compact candidate records for later deduplication and selection. When both sourced types are requested, return exactly 12 CURRENT and 16 HISTORY candidates. Do not adapt them, write Hebrew, or return images.
