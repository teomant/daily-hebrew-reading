# Adaptation and annotation instructions

This phase receives frozen story briefs and metadata. Do not search for topics, choose different stories, change metadata, or apply cross-day novelty rules.

Follow this priority order:

1. Return a complete, usable adaptation for every requested story and every configured level.
2. Preserve the frozen story, required HISTORY beats, correct Hebrew, and required DIALOG structure.
3. Add useful lexical segmentation and contextual translations.

Publishing a usable article is the goal. Segmentation is best-effort annotation and must never replace, shorten, or prevent the return of an otherwise usable article. When a boundary is uncertain, prefer smaller single-word or short-phrase units and continue.

Write modern spoken Israeli Hebrew: natural language a contemporary adult might use to tell, ask, answer, clarify, or explain something to another person. Favor reusable conversational sentences over news-style narration. Avoid biblical, religious, literary, bureaucratic, specialist, and dense newspaper language. Do not use niqqud. Keep one central idea and develop it with distinct supported details; include interaction or outcome when the material calls for it, not as an invented requirement. Never repeat sentences merely to reach a word target.

Use normal sentence punctuation in every version. Every teaser and every body paragraph or DIALOG turn must end with `.` `?` `!` or `…`. Use commas and internal sentence boundaries wherever natural; do not return an unpunctuated block of Hebrew.

Create every configured reading level from the same frozen factual or scenario material. For HISTORY, the compact `brief` identifies the subject, while `storyBeats` and `_storyBeatContract` provide the authoritative facts. Develop the defining facts and interesting details into a readable article. It may explain a person, custom, cultural work, industry, place, object, or past event without forcing a beginning–turning-point–outcome plot. Do not write a summary of the source, a bare résumé, promotional copy, or generic commentary about importance, influence, legacy, society, culture, memory, or how history feels. Phrases such as “the story shows,” “his life teaches,” and “the factory reflects society” cannot replace concrete facts.

For CURRENT, HISTORY, and full EVERYDAY stories, write 4–5 real paragraphs with multiple sentences when the frozen material supports them. Give each paragraph a distinct job in developing the central idea; for an informative HISTORY article, organize related facts into a clear progression of ideas without inventing a plot. DIALOG uses the separate turn-per-paragraph rule below, and `_shortItem` uses its compact one-paragraph rule from the request. Every HISTORY level must preserve the required defining facts. Easier levels may compress secondary dates, names, qualifiers, or context and use simpler syntax; they must not replace the subject with generalities. Harder versions may express the same material with broader vocabulary, more natural connectors, common fixed expressions, and moderately richer sentence structure, but the facts must remain consistent. Do not make a harder level longer merely to signal difficulty.

For Alef, use common concrete words, active clauses, simple verb forms, and one event or thought per short sentence. Avoid passive constructions, abstract nominal phrases, formal connectors, unnecessary foreign terms, dense construct chains, and nested clauses. Explain a necessary less-common term immediately in ordinary words. For example, replace `בעקבות העיכוב בקבלת המשלוח, התקבלה החלטה על שינוי מועד הפגישה` with `המשלוח הגיע מאוחר. בגלל זה הם החליטו להעביר את הפגישה.`

Treat Alef as end-of-Alef reading, not a shorter Bet article. Draft it as a standalone retelling before looking at the harder versions. Use familiar verbs and direct subjects; give each short sentence one action or thought. Replace an abstract noun with the concrete action when the facts allow it. Read the continuous Hebrew aloud before segmenting, and simplify any sentence that makes the reader hold several new facts at once.

For Alef HISTORY, include every required defining fact. Omit optional beats that add names, dates, or specialist detail without helping the reader; include a few easy optional facts when they make the article substantial, clear, or enjoyable. Keep the central person, place, custom, work, or event identifiable. Do not turn research into a chronology or a list of dates, other names, institutions, titles, numbers, academic fields, or technical terms. Include such a detail only when the reader needs it; explain an essential unfamiliar term in a short adjacent sentence with everyday words. Preserve what makes the subject specific rather than replacing it with generic significance. Do not repeat a fact to fill the paragraph count, and report coverage only for beats actually retold.

For CURRENT, retain the specific supported event or subject and its distinguishing details at every level. Preserve a practical consequence when the source supports one, but do not invent one for a human-interest or culture article. A date, location, person, action, or next step that distinguishes the item should survive simplification when the frozen brief supplies it. Do not turn a concrete notice into four paragraphs that only repeat that something changed and readers should check for updates.

Write and proofread each version as continuous Hebrew before any lexical annotation is attempted. Keep the amount of content appropriate to the format and supplied material. Never invent factual claims, add irrelevant background, repeat the same point, or make sentences unnaturally verbose. Do not count words or force one reading level to be longer than another.

For each returned story, include `coveredStoryBeatIds` keyed by every reading-level ID. For HISTORY, list every beat ID actually represented in that level and include all beats marked `required`; never claim coverage for an omitted fact. For CURRENT, EVERYDAY, and DIALOG, return an empty list for every level. Coverage metadata is validation-only and is not published.

For DIALOG, write only direct speech and return 8–12 short turns at every level. “Short” means a natural conversational turn, often one or two useful sentences, not a fragment that strips out the planned interaction. Use follow-up questions, answers, clarification, reaction, and agreement or another practical outcome to develop the exchange. Every paragraph-array item must contain exactly one complete speaker turn so the renderer displays every turn on a separate line. Start each item with one consistent Hebrew speaker name and a colon, such as `נועה: ...` or `דני: ...`. Never place two speaker labels or two speakers in the same paragraph item. Alternate naturally between at least two speakers. Keep the names and colons visible. Do not add third-person narration such as “נועה ראתה”, “היא שאלה”, “הם בדקו”, or “בסוף הם החליטו”. Put necessary context and actions into what the speakers say, and never use long speeches merely to reach the word target.

During the segmentation phase, treat the supplied Hebrew wording and punctuation as immutable. Apply the same chunk-size guidance to titles, teasers, and paragraphs. Prefer one to three Hebrew words per meaningful lexical unit. Keep a longer fixed expression, proper name, or indivisible phrase together only when splitting it would make the meaning worse. Never put a complete sentence into one translation target; split it into useful words and short phrases at natural grammatical boundaries. If unsure, split more rather than returning the sentence as one unit.

Literal segmentation anti-example based on a bad production result:

- BAD: one meaningful unit containing `המשטרה עצרה שלושה חשודים.`
- GOOD: four units: `המשטרה` / `עצרה` / `שלושה חשודים` / `.`. The first three are meaningful units with contextual translations; the final period is a separator with empty translations.

The same rule applies to headings. BAD: one unit containing `מעצרים אחרי פריצות לבתי ספר באזור לכיש`. GOOD: `מעצרים` / `אחרי פריצות` / `לבתי ספר` / `באזור לכיש`.

Keep attached Hebrew prefixes inside their complete written word; never emit forms such as `ב העיר`, `מ מקום`, or `ל רחוב`. Preserve expressions such as מזג אוויר, שם לב, and בסופו של דבר as single units when splitting would harm understanding. Do not emit separator units for ordinary single spaces between meaningful units; the renderer restores those spaces locally. Use separator units only for punctuation or whitespace whose exact placement matters. Never return a lexical unit with an empty `text` value; omit it instead.

After annotation, reconstruct every title, teaser, and paragraph by joining its lexical units exactly as the reader will see them, then proofread again. Confirm that no words were glued together or split apart, attached prefixes remain attached, language-bearing text such as `של`, `לו`, `את`, or a detached `ו` was not classified as a separator, punctuation is present, agreement and prepositions are grammatical, and the Hebrew sounds natural rather than translated word for word. Fix the units before returning the response; segmentation must never damage the Hebrew you wrote.

Translate every meaningful unit in every configured translation language whenever a useful translation exists. Read the whole sentence before translating a unit. Translate its contextual meaning and grammatical role—including attached Hebrew prefixes, tense, reference, and idiomatic meaning—not merely its isolated dictionary form. Use an empty string only when the unit genuinely has no useful direct translation in context; never stop translating because enough other units have translations. Separators have empty translations.

Return only data matching the supplied schema. Return every requested story ID exactly once and no other IDs. Teasers must be one concise sentence. Do not put Markdown or HTML in content fields.
