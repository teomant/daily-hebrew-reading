# Selected HISTORY research instructions

Research only the already selected HISTORY subjects supplied by the user request. This phase decides whether each subject supports a substantive historical reading article and freezes sourced factual material for later Hebrew adaptation. Do not write Hebrew, learner-level prose, translations, summaries of source articles, or promotional copy.

Use web search substantially for every subject, in Hebrew as well as English when relevant. Begin with its screening lead when one exists, then search beyond it for additional specific material about the same subject. For Israeli subjects, consider the National Library of Israel, Historical Jewish Press, Israel State Archives, National Photo Collection, PikiWiki Israel, Israel Film Archive, Project Ben-Yehuda, Hebrew and English Wikipedia references, official and municipal archives, museums' collection records, university and educational collections, biographies, and reputable Israeli reporting. No particular collection is mandatory; choose sources that fit the subject. For a worldwide fallback subject, use the strongest appropriate primary, archival, institutional, and biographical sources. Prefer material with concrete facts about the subject's work, characteristics, history, use, setting, changes, and significance. Source links are optional output metadata: return trustworthy canonical HTTPS content pages when available, but do not reject an otherwise usable subject merely because no usable URL can be returned. Never invent a link. Do not use homepages, section pages, search pages, generic latest pages, or rolling liveblogs.

A subject is sufficient when the collected material supports an interesting, specific article with distinct facts an adult learner can follow. A human or institutional sequence is useful when it exists, but not mandatory: a historical politician, holiday, cultural work, trade, object, or place can be explained through several concrete facets. A source page, archive record, photograph, museum object, topic label, résumé, event listing, exhibition description, or generic claim about importance is not itself enough; research the underlying subject.

Mark a current exhibition, festival listing, anniversary program, promotional institutional feature, or shallow profile as insufficient unless independent research reveals substantive historical facts that stand without the current promotion. Never pad a thin subject with generic statements that it shows, reflects, represents, symbolizes, preserves memory, shaped culture, or remains important.

For every sufficient subject, return 8–12 non-overlapping English factual beats. Give each beat a stable ID and descriptive role; use `detail` when a narrative role does not fit. Mark at least two defining facts as required so every level remains recognizably about the same subject. Do not mark every beat required: secondary dates, names, numbers, and specialist details should usually remain optional for Alef. The factual beats can develop distinct facets rather than a plot. `supportingSourceUrls` may be empty; when URLs are available, use exact URLs from the returned source list. Do not infer beyond the researched material or invent quotations, motives, reactions, dates, numbers, causes, or consequences.

If the researched material cannot satisfy the 8–12-beat factual contract, return the subject as insufficient with a concise reason and empty beats. Missing source links alone never makes a subject insufficient. It is better to release a genuinely thin slot to an already reviewed reserve than to manufacture a summary-shaped article.
